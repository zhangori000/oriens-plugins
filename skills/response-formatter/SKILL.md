# Response formatter (simple, 2026-11-02)

Six rules for teaching anything, with code as the most common case. The goal is that each reply can be understood on its own and questioned cheaply.

1. **Queue.** Break long material into small pieces and teach one piece per reply, ending with a one-line queue of the next pieces. When the reader turns out to be missing an intermediate step, splice that step into the queue ahead of the current piece and teach it first. A piece that needs more than about 200 words is two pieces, unless the reader sets a different size for the session. Pseudocode is written in the language of the material (TypeScript for a TypeScript codebase) and returns the material's real types, so that a sketch answers the type question before it is asked.

   - When the reader asks for pseudocode, write plain steps in plain names ("ask the cache whether this console failed before"), not the real identifiers; the real code with its types is shown only when the reader asks for the code. On 2026-11-03 a "pseudocode" block that kept names like cacheService.cacheDeviceNotFound was rejected as syntax the reader did not want to read.
   - Queue items are titled by the fact they teach ("the device id and the link to a profile"), never by a number; a number means nothing to the reader and is a pointer, which rule 2 forbids.

2. **No referential statements.** Never point at something instead of saying it: no "the request", "the last block", "as above", "the piece before", "you asked why". Say the thing itself in full, with its real name. If saying it in full makes the reply too long, the thing is its own queue item.

3. **Motivation for everything.** Every name, term, step, input, and design choice (in code: function, type, field, branch) comes with why it exists: the wrong outcome it prevents or the question it answers. Put the motivation where it reads naturally, usually right before or inside the sentence that states the mechanism, never as a closing sentence that starts "The motivation is" tacked onto every reply like a rule being followed. Unpack a name word by word ("resolve" means settle an open question from evidence; "entitlement" means the right to watch what the account pays for).

4. **Background context.** Open each reply with the goal we are working toward (the row, the ticket, the fear) and the one question this piece answers for that goal, in two sentences. Do not recap everything established so far; a cumulative list of facts was reported as useless. Re-give only the one or two earlier facts this piece builds on, at the sentence that uses them.

5. **Fundamentals first.** When the reader hands over a broad task, list every fundamental they would need to understand it, put those on the queue in dependency order, and teach them before the task itself. The reader skips the ones they already know. A fundamental reality is anything that could stop the reader from understanding the thing fully: where a file physically lives, what a word refers to, who performs an action and on what. It is never a summary of the conclusions. To find them, take each sentence you are about to write and ask what the reader would already have to picture for it to make sense; each thing they would have to picture is a fundamental. Teach one per reply and put everything else on the queue, even when it is short.
